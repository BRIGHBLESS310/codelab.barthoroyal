Start with a user problem

A strong AI feature begins with a specific user problem, not a model or vendor. Describe who needs help, what they are trying to do, and how the experience should improve.

Key idea

Compare an AI proposal with a simpler baseline such as search, a form, or a deterministic rule. Define an outcome you can observe and a consequence if the system is wrong.

Translate the need into a measurable task

Problem framing starts by describing the user's current difficulty, not by naming a model or asking for an AI feature. Identify who experiences the problem, in what context, how it is handled today, and what outcome would count as a real improvement.

Then define the system's role and boundary: what input it receives, what it may return, and what remains a human decision. Make success observable, such as a reduction in time without an unacceptable rise in errors. This creates a basis for a baseline and later evaluation.

Consider the costs of both correct and incorrect results, including the people who may be excluded or burdened. If a simple interface, search, or deterministic rule solves the need more safely, use that instead. A tightly framed problem keeps experimentation focused and makes an eventual stop decision possible.

Example

The baseline is simple and measurable. It may solve enough of the problem without introducing the complexity of generated answers.

user_need = "Find the right policy page quickly"
baseline = "Search approved policy titles"
measure = "time to a useful, correct page"

Practice

Write a problem statement for an AI-assisted help feature. Include the user, task, baseline, success measure, and a reason not to use AI.

Try the example, change one detail, and explain how the result changes.
